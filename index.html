Daily schedule

4 cups of food daily3 feedings4 bathroom outings
  1. First thing in the morning, take her outside.
  2. Second outing about 4–5 hours later.
  3. Third outing another 4–5 hours later.
  4. Final outing before bedtime. No meal immediately before bed.

Repeat the cycle each day. Take her out sooner when she signals she needs to go.

Feeding & rewards

4 cups total per day, split into 3 feedings: 1⅓ cups each. Her meals are part of her coming-in-and-going-out routine.

At home

After she uses the bathroom, she comes inside and eats. If she eats when she comes in, no extra bathroom treat is necessary.

On public walks

When she uses the bathroom, praise her and give her treats.

Never reach for her food. She may snap.

Bathroom & leash routine

  1. At a new house, take her inside first. Remove her leash and place it in a consistent, accessible spot.
  2. Ask her to retrieve the leash and say “Outside.” She can bring it to your hand.
  3. Put on her leash and take her to the designated bathroom spot.
  4. Say “Get busy, get busy.”
  5. When she goes, say “Good girl.” At home, her meal on returning inside can be her reward. On public walks, give treats.
  6. Return the leash to its spot and practice this two or three times on the first day. She can learn to bring her leash to request an outing.

Continue offering regular outings even after she learns to signal.

Say “Look” to gain attention. Treats, hand gestures, and a gentle hindquarter scratch can help. If she is looking at you but not sitting, give a thumbs-up gesture.

Speak confidently and clearly. Reserve hard yelling for urgent safety situations.

Walking & other animals

Keep her securely leashed. She has a strong prey drive, especially around squirrels, and may expect to chase because her owner has permitted it. Do not repeat off-leash obedience tests.

If she focuses on birds and her hackles rise, tell her “Assis” and regain her attention. Avoid confrontations with raccoons, cats, or other animals that could hurt her. If she reacts to another dog, create distance and redirect.

On public walks, give treats after she uses the bathroom.

Food & mouth safety

DO NOT REACH FOR HER FOOD. SHE MAY SNAP.

Her owner describes her as bite-trained and permits certain mouth contact in their own interactions. Caregivers should not test this. If her teeth touch you, stay calm, stop the interaction, and do not put your hand back toward her mouth or food.

To retrieve an object, offer a higher-value treat and ask her for it. She is fetch-trained and may hand it over or drop it. Prefer letting her drop it; do not pry open her mouth or take it by force.

At home & visitors

Hashebrowne is generally quiet, but may bark at knocks, unfamiliar visitors, arrivals, or delivery drivers. Secure her before opening the door, use a calm voice, and redirect her attention.
